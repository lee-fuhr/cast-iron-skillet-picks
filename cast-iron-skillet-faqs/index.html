Cast Iron Skillet questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Cast iron skillets are beloved for their heat retention, durability, and versatility, but choosing and caring for one comes with common questions. This guide covers the basics, from sizing and seasoning to cleaning and cooktop compatibility, so you can buy and use one with confidence.

What makes cast iron skillets so popular?

Cast iron skillets have held a spot in kitchens for generations because they do things other pans can’t. Their thick walls and even heating make them ideal for searing steaks, baking cornbread, or frying chicken. Once properly seasoned, they develop a natural non-stick surface that improves with use. Beyond cooking performance, cast iron is nearly indestructible. A well-maintained skillet can last decades, even centuries, and be passed down. It’s also one of the few pans that works on any heat source: gas, electric, induction, and even campfires. The trade-off is weight; these pans are heavy, and the handle gets hot. But for many home cooks, the versatility and durability outweigh those minor hassles.

How do I choose the right size cast iron skillet?

The most common sizes are 10-inch and 12-inch skillets, but you’ll also find 8-inch and 14-inch options. A 10-inch skillet is a great all-rounder for two to four people, good for eggs, pancakes, and small roasts. A 12-inch gives you more surface area for searing multiple steaks or cooking a full meal of chicken and vegetables. Think about your stove burners and oven space. A very large skillet might not fit in a standard home oven, and it can be unwieldy to lift when full. If you’re cooking for one or two and have limited storage, a 10-inch is plenty. For families or batch cooking, the 12-inch is worth the extra weight. Many collectors own both sizes. Also consider handle length and helper handles, some skillets have a small loop opposite the main handle, making it easier to lift with two hands. That’s a nice feature in larger sizes.

What is seasoning and do I need to do it?

Seasoning is a layer of polymerized oil that bonds to the iron, creating a smooth, non-stick surface and protecting the metal from rust. Most new cast iron skillets come pre-seasoned from the factory, brands like Lodge apply a vegetable-oil seasoning that is ready to use. But that initial layer is thin, and it builds up over time with regular cooking. You don’t need to season a new skillet unless it’s raw (unseasoned) iron. For pre-seasoned pans, just start cooking. The seasoning strengthens naturally when you use oils or fats and avoid prolonged boiling of acidic foods. If the surface ever looks dry or patchy, you can apply a very thin coat of oil and heat the pan in the oven for an hour. That’s called reseasoning, and it’s easy to do at home. Mistakes happen: burned-on food, soap that stripped the layer, or rust from moisture. Don’t panic. A light scrub and a fresh round of seasoning usually restores performance.

Can I use cast iron on any cooktop?

Yes, cast iron works on all standard cooktops: gas, electric coil, ceramic glass, and induction. Because the pan is heavy and has a flat bottom, it sits nicely on glass tops, but you must lift it, never slide or drag, to avoid scratching. On induction, cast iron is ideal because it’s magnetic and heats up evenly, though you may need to heat it slowly to avoid hot spots. The only real limitation is very old or delicate cooktops where rough handling could cause damage, but that’s a caution about lifting, not compatibility. Cast iron also excels over campfires and on grills, making it a flexible choice for outdoor cooking as well.

How do I clean and care for a cast iron skillet?

Basic care is simpler than many people think. After cooking, let the pan cool slightly, then rinse under hot water and scrub with a stiff brush or non-abrasive pad. A small amount of mild dish soap is fine for removing stuck-on food, modern soap won’t ruin a well-seasoned pan. Dry it immediately with a towel, then heat it on the stove for a minute to evaporate any remaining moisture. Avoid soaking the pan for long periods, and never put it in the dishwasher. That will strip the seasoning and cause rust. If food sticks badly, you can boil a little water in the pan to loosen it, then scrub. Once clean, rub a very thin layer of cooking oil over the interior to refresh the seasoning. For rust, scrub it off with steel wool, dry completely, and apply a thin coat of oil. Then bake the skillet upside down in a 400°F (200°C) oven for an hour. Your seasoning will be back in business.

What are the best cast iron skillet brands?

Lodge is the most widely available and budget-friendly option. Their pre-seasoned skillets are reliable and heavy-duty, though they have a slightly rougher cooking surface compared to pricier brands. For a smoother finish, Le Creuset offers enameled cast iron that doesn’t require seasoning and is easier to clean, but it costs significantly more. Smaller American makers like Field Company and Stargazer produce lighter skillets with polished interiors and ergonomic handles. They’re noticeably easier to handle than traditional Lodge pans, and the surface becomes more non-stick faster. These brands are priced higher, roughly in the mid-range to premium category, but many enthusiasts find the weight reduction worth it. Vintage cast iron (like Griswold or Wagner) is also highly sought after for its thin, smooth cooking surface. You can find these at antique shops or online auctions. They work just as well as new pans, but you’ll need to inspect for cracks or warping. No matter which brand you choose, all cast iron will serve you well if you care for it properly.

Frequently asked questions

Can I put cast iron in the dishwasher?

No. Dishwasher detergent is too harsh and will strip the seasoning, and the prolonged wet environment promotes rust. Always hand wash and dry thoroughly.

Is it safe to cook acidic foods in cast iron?

Yes, but only for short periods. Prolonged cooking of acidic ingredients like tomatoes or vinegar can react with the iron and give food a metallic taste, as well as damage the seasoning. Keep such dishes under 30 minutes, or use an enameled cast iron pan instead.

Do I need to preheat cast iron before cooking?

Yes, always preheat gradually. Start on low heat and let the pan warm up for 3–5 minutes, then increase to your desired temperature. Sudden high heat can warp the pan or cause hot spots that lead to sticking.

Can I use metal utensils on cast iron?

Yes, metal utensils are safe, but they can scrape off the seasoning if you’re aggressive. Wooden or silicone utensils are gentler and help preserve that non-stick layer. Over time, minor scratches from metal usually heal with use.

How do I remove rust from a cast iron skillet?

Scrub the rust off with steel wool or a stiff brush and hot water. Dry the pan completely, then apply a very thin coat of cooking oil. Place it upside down in a 400°F (200°C) oven for one hour to reseason. Repeat if needed.

Is cast iron truly non-stick?

When well-seasoned, cast iron can be very non-stick, enough for eggs and delicate fish, but it’s not as slick as modern chemical non-stick coatings. The more you cook with oils and fats, the better the seasoning becomes, reducing sticking over time.

In shortCast iron isn’t a set-it-and-forget-it purchase, but the small learning curve pays off with decades of excellent cooking. Start with a size that fits your kitchen and cooking style, treat the seasoning as a living surface, and don’t be afraid to scrub and reseason when needed. A good cast iron skillet is a partnership, the more you use it, the better it gets.
