How to choose the right cast iron skillet

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The best cast iron skillet for most people in 2026 remains a pre-seasoned Lodge, because it’s affordable, durable, and widely available. If you want a lighter, smoother-cooking pan, the enameled models from Le Creuset or Staub are worth the higher cost, but they require gentler care. Focus on size (10 to 12 inches is the kitchen workhorse), weight, and handle comfort, and never fear the seasoning myth.

Why buy a cast iron skillet in 2026?

Cast iron skillets have been around for centuries because they do one thing brilliantly: hold steady, even heat. That makes them perfect for searing steaks, baking cornbread, frying chicken, or simmering sauces. Modern options are better than ever, with pre-seasoned surfaces reducing the learning curve and enameled models eliminating the need to season at all. There’s also a sustainability angle. A well-maintained cast iron pan can outlast you, which means fewer kitchen disposables in landfills. The nonstick properties are naturally derived from polymerized oil, not synthetic coatings. If you cook frequently and want one pan that does almost everything, cast iron is a smart investment.

What size cast iron skillet should you choose?

The 10-inch skillet (often called a medium skillet) is the most versatile for one or two people. It handles two chicken breasts, a small frittata, or a skillet cookie easily. A 12-inch skillet is the sweet spot for families of three to four: it fits four hamburger patties, a whole chicken, or a full batch of cornbread. Larger skillets, like 14 or 15 inches, are heavy and can be awkward to lift for many cooks. Smaller 8-inch pans are great for eggs or individual servings, but they have less cooking surface. Unless you often cook for a crowd, a 10- or 12-inch pan is the practical choice.

Pre-seasoned vs. enameled cast iron: which is right for you?

Pre-seasoned skillets, like those from Lodge, come with a layer of baked-on oil that naturally builds into a nonstick patina with use. They’re inexpensive, tough, and can handle metal utensils or high heat. The trade-off is that they’re reactive with acidic foods (tomatoes, wine) and require ongoing oiling after each wash. Enameled cast iron has a glass-like coating that never rusts, never needs seasoning, and is safe with acidic ingredients. Brands like Le Creuset and Staub dominate here. They’re significantly more expensive, heavier, and the enamel can chip if dropped or overheated. Choose enameled if you want low upkeep and cook with acidic foods often; choose pre-seasoned if you want a budget-friendly, near-indestructible pan.

What features matter most for comfort and durability?

Weight is the biggest practical concern. A 12-inch pre-seasoned pan can weigh around 7 to 8 pounds, which gets heavy when you’re flipping or pouring. Look for pans with a long, comfortable handle (often cast as one piece) that stays cool enough to grip for a few seconds. Some brands add a helper handle on the opposite side for two-handed lifting. Wall height matters too: deeper walls (2 to 3 inches) prevent splatter and make shallow frying easier, while flared sides make flipping easier. Also, check for a smooth cooking surface, some cheaper pans have a rough texture that can improve with seasoning, but smoother surfaces are easier to clean and more nonstick from day one.

How to take care of a cast iron skillet without the hype

Ignore the old rule about never using soap. A little mild dish soap and a soft sponge will not ruin seasoning. Dry the pan thoroughly on the stove over low heat, then rub a thin layer of cooking oil over the surface before storing. That’s it. For stuck-on food, scrub with coarse salt and a little oil or use a plastic scraper. Never soak a pre-seasoned pan, but enameled pans can soak briefly. If rust appears, scrub it off with steel wool, re-season in the oven, and carry on. A spatula with a metal edge is fine; enameled pans require wood or silicone to avoid chipping.

Which brands are worth your money in 2026?

Lodge remains the default choice for budget-minded cooks. Their Logic line is pre-seasoned, widely available, and extremely durable. If you want a lighter pan, consider the Field Company or Smith & Clark, they’re thinner, smoother, and easier to handle, but cost more than Lodge. For enameled, Le Creuset and Staub are the standards. Le Creuset has a lighter-colored interior that lets you see fond, while Staub has a black matte interior that holds heat longer. Both are lifetime purchases if cared for. The cheaper enameled options from Crock‑Pot or Cuisinart can work, but the enamel may wear quicker. Stick with a known brand and check return policies for chips.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use cast iron on an induction cooktop?

Yes, cast iron works on induction because it’s magnetic. Just be careful not to slide the pan across the glass surface, as it can scratch. Lift it instead.

Is it safe to cook tomatoes or wine-based sauces in cast iron?

In a pre-seasoned pan, acidic foods can react with the iron, giving a metallic taste and damaging seasoning. Enameled cast iron is the better choice for these dishes. If you only cook acidic foods occasionally, a quick cook (under 30 minutes) is usually fine.

How do I strip old seasoning and start fresh?

Use the oven’s self-cleaning cycle (outdoors if possible, due to smoke) or spray the pan with oven cleaner and let it sit overnight in a plastic bag. Then wash and re-season with a thin layer of oil heated at 400°F for an hour. Never use a grinding tool; it ruins the surface.

What’s the best oil for seasoning?

Vegetable oils with high smoke points, such as canola, grapeseed, or flaxseed oil, work well. Flaxseed gives a hard, dark coating but can flake over time. Many users prefer canola or grapeseed for the best balance of durability and ease. Avoid butter or olive oil for seasoning, they burn too quickly.

Do I need to preheat a cast iron skillet?

Yes, always preheat on medium heat for 3–5 minutes before adding oil or food. This prevents sticking and ensures even cooking. Never start on high unless you’re searing after the pan is already hot. Gradual heating prevents warping.

Can I put cast iron in the dishwasher?

No. Dishwasher detergents are too harsh and will strip seasoning from a pre-seasoned pan. Enameled pans may get dull or chipped from the harsh spray. Always hand wash with warm water and mild soap.

In shortA cast iron skillet isn’t a mysterious kitchen relic, it’s a straightforward tool that rewards simple, consistent care. Focus on the size that fits your cooking habits, decide whether you want pre-seasoned or enameled, and don’t overthink the maintenance. A well-chosen skillet will outlive every other pan in your cupboard, and your grandchildren might still be using it.
